What we mean by “interactivity”

Six small things that change a markdown document from a static page to a place to read. Each one shown side-by-side: how it looks without the feature, and how it looks with it.

01

Paragraphs appear one by one

Instead of the whole document slamming into view at once, the paragraphs cascade in — each one starts about 100ms after the previous. Subtle, but it gives your eye a path to follow.

Without — everything at once

The compiler is not a magic box. It reads your code, checks the rules, and produces a smaller version.

Most errors come from misunderstanding what the compiler is allowed to assume.

When in doubt, read the error message twice.

With — paragraphs cascade in

The compiler is not a magic box. It reads your code, checks the rules, and produces a smaller version.

Most errors come from misunderstanding what the compiler is allowed to assume.

When in doubt, read the error message twice.

Why When everything appears simultaneously, your eye doesn’t know where to start. When paragraphs arrive in sequence, your eye follows naturally. You notice the effect on day one, forget it by week two — but you’d miss it if it were gone.
02

The paragraph you’re reading stays bright

The other paragraphs fade gently so they don’t compete for your attention. Hover over each paragraph in the right-hand box to feel it.

Without — all equally lit

Reading is not just decoding letters; it is also choosing what to look at.

On a long page with many paragraphs, the eye tires from constant choice.

Anything that reduces that choice without removing context earns its place.

With — hover any line

Reading is not just decoding letters; it is also choosing what to look at.

On a long page with many paragraphs, the eye tires from constant choice.

Anything that reduces that choice without removing context earns its place.

Why On long documents you can lose your place. Dimming the surroundings gives your eye a quiet zone — like a spotlight, but for prose. In the real app this triggers on scroll, not hover.
03

Notes appear in the margin, not at the bottom

A footnote-style reference (the small 1) is followed not by a jump to the end of the page, but by a note in the right margin right next to where you are.

Without — footnote at bottom

Markdown was designed to be readable as plain text1. It is the lowest-friction writing format that has ever existed.

You have to scroll down to read the footnote — losing your place each time.

1 John Gruber, 2004. The original spec is still under 1,000 words.
With — note in the side margin

Markdown was designed to be readable as plain text1. It is the lowest-friction writing format that has ever existed.

The note is right there — you don’t lose your place.

1. John Gruber, 2004. The original spec is still under 1,000 words.
Why Footnotes are an old solution to a paper problem (the page is bound; the note has to go somewhere fixed). On screen, the margin is free real estate. The note arrives without taking you away from the sentence.
04

Tips, warnings, and notes look different from regular text

Instead of being buried in prose with a bold “Note:” prefix, they get a colored box and an icon so the reader spots them at a glance.

Without — buried in text

Warning: Make sure to back up your database before running this migration. It cannot be undone without a restore.

Tip: You can preview the migration with the --dry-run flag.

With — colored boxes
Warning · Make sure to back up your database before running this migration. It cannot be undone without a restore.
Tip · You can preview the migration with the --dry-run flag.
Why Different kinds of information should look different. A warning in plain prose disappears; a warning in a colored box gets the right amount of attention — without shouting.
05

Code blocks have a copy button

Click “Copy” on the right-hand block. It turns green for a moment. One click instead of select-and-copy.

Without — highlight to copy
bash
$ npm install && npm run build
With — one-click copy
bash 
$ npm install && npm run build
Why If your document has commands, examples, or snippets, copying them is the main thing readers will do. Saving three keystrokes per copy adds up across a long document.
06

A thin bar shows how far through you are

As you scroll, a thin line at the top fills. Scroll inside the right-hand box to see it move.

Without — no indicator

The pace of reading is not the pace of writing.

A page that takes ten minutes to write may take thirty seconds to read.

Without an indicator, the reader has no sense of pacing.

This makes long documents feel longer than they are.

Anxiety creeps in: am I almost done? How much more?

The mind starts skimming.

You arrive at the end and don’t remember the middle.

A progress indicator solves all of this with three pixels of height.

With — progress at the top

The pace of reading is not the pace of writing.

A page that takes ten minutes to write may take thirty seconds to read.

With an indicator, the reader pacings themselves.

You always know what fraction is left.

Anxiety drops. The mind relaxes into the prose.

You arrive at the end having actually read the middle.

Three pixels of height. Huge perceived difference.

The same trick works for chapters, sections, or whole books.

Why Like a film’s runtime indicator, knowing “I’m a third through” helps you pace yourself. Without it, long documents feel infinite.

That’s the whole idea.

Six small things. Each one fewer than fifty lines of code. The markdown source you write stays plain text — portable, diffable, future-proof. What changes is how it’s rendered. The reader gets a document that feels like a place; you still have plain text underneath. That’s the synthesis.
